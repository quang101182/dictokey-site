Reviews

1. DictoKey

DictoKey gives you two independent ways to dictate: a standard Android keyboard (navigable with a screen reader) and a floating bubble above most apps. If one is not available, the other still works. Clean mode restores punctuation and capitals, so a dictated message rarely needs retouching.

Translation covers 51 languages, with an Interpreter Mode for spoken conversations (free users can try it 3 times a day). You can rewrite text with AI, say a correction from the keyboard, and let the app detect the language. If a dictation cannot be inserted, DictoKey keeps it in memory for a few minutes and offers to insert it in the next field you tap. Speech recognition runs in the cloud, so you need internet. It is GDPR compliant, stores no recordings and shows no ads; banking apps, password managers and crypto wallets are blocked automatically. Free: 30 dictations a day, no account. Premium: about $7.50/month or about $55 per year, 7-day free trial for new subscribers, 1,000 dictations a day.

Limits: Android and Windows only (no iOS), internet required, and 51 languages is fewer than the 100+ some vendors claim.

Verdict: a complete choice if you want dictation, translation and rewriting in one app.

2. Gboard

Gboard is Google's keyboard, with a microphone key for voice typing. Nothing to install on most phones, and it is free. Its strength is moving between voice and typing in the same field. We did not verify its language count, offline behavior or any translation feature today. See Gboard voice typing alternatives and what to do when it misses words.

Verdict: the sensible default for short messages.

3. Microsoft SwiftKey

SwiftKey is known for predictive typing that learns your style, and it includes voice input. It suits people who mostly type and occasionally dictate. We did not verify its current language or price details today.

Verdict: pick it for typing prediction, not dictation-first work.

4. Typeless

Typeless's pricing page lists Android among its platforms. The Free plan has “limited words” reset weekly (no figure given); Pro is $12 per month billed yearly or $30 per month billed monthly. It advertises support for 100+ languages on both plans. We did not verify offline support.

Verdict: worth a look if you want a dedicated voice keyboard with a free tier to test.

5. Wispr Flow

Wispr's pricing page lists dictation on Mac, Windows, iOS and Android. Free shows “limited weekly use on mobile”; Pro is $15 per user per month, or $12 per month billed annually, and the page advertises 100+ languages. It is popular for cleaning up long-form dictation. See our Wispr Flow alternative page.

Verdict: strong for long-form writing if the free weekly limit and Pro price suit you.

6. Yaps

Yaps's pricing page says voice typing is “free on Android, iPhone, and iPad, no account or card needed” and that the free plan runs on-device. Its FAQ says core dictation works offline once local models are installed. Paid Pro adds desktop apps ($144 per year, about $12 a month; the page also mentions $15 monthly). It does not state its language list there.

Verdict: the pick if offline use and on-device privacy matter most; it is also free.

Who should pick what

  • Quick messages at no cost: Gboard.
  • Offline, on-device dictation: Yaps.
  • Mostly typing, sometimes dictating: SwiftKey.
  • Long-form dictation you also do on desktop: Wispr Flow or Typeless.
  • Translation, rewriting, and a bubble plus keyboard: DictoKey.
