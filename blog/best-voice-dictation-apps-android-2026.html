Reviews

1. DictoKey

DictoKey gives you two independent ways to dictate: a standard Android keyboard (navigable with a screen reader) and a floating bubble above most apps. If one is not available, the other still works. Clean mode restores punctuation and capitals, so a dictated message rarely needs retouching.

Translation covers 51 languages, with an Interpreter Mode for spoken conversations (free users can try it 3 times a day). You can rewrite text with AI, say a correction from the keyboard, and let the app detect the language. Speech recognition runs in the cloud, so you need an internet connection. It is GDPR compliant, stores no recordings and shows no ads; banking apps, password managers and crypto wallets are blocked automatically. Free: 30 dictations a day, no account. Premium: about $7.50/month or about $55 per year, 7-day free trial for new subscribers, 1,000 dictations a day.

Limits: Android only (no iOS), internet required, and 51 languages is fewer than some built-in options.

Verdict: a complete choice if you want dictation, translation and rewriting in one app.

2. Google voice typing

Voice typing is the microphone key already present on most Android keyboards, so there is nothing to install. It is free and the fastest way to start. We did not verify its language count, offline behavior or editing commands today, so check Google's own help pages if those matter.

Verdict: the sensible default for short messages. Stuck with it? See Gboard voice typing alternatives.

3. Gboard

Gboard is Google's keyboard, with voice input as one feature among many (swipe typing, emoji, clipboard). Its strength is that you can move between voice and typing in the same field. It is free. Voice is not its specialty, and we found no inline voice translation or AI rewriting in the sources we checked.

Verdict: a solid free keyboard if voice is occasional.

4. Microsoft SwiftKey

SwiftKey is known for predictive typing that learns your writing style. Voice dictation is available but is not its main focus, so it suits people who mostly type and occasionally dictate. We did not verify its current language or price details today.

Verdict: pick it for typing prediction, not for dictation-first work.

5. Wispr Flow

Wispr Flow lists dictation on Android on its pricing page, alongside Mac, Windows and iOS. Its Free plan shows “limited weekly use on mobile”; Pro is $15 per user per month, or $12 per month billed annually, and it advertises 100+ languages. It is popular for cleaning up long-form dictation. See our Wispr Flow alternative page for a closer comparison.

Verdict: a strong pick for long-form writing if the free weekly limit and Pro price suit you.

6. Otter.ai

Otter.ai is a meeting transcription service: recorder, searchable transcripts and summaries. It is a standalone app rather than a keyboard, so it belongs to a different category than everyday dictation. We did not verify its current plans or languages today.

Verdict: use it for meetings, not for typing messages.

7. Speechnotes

Speechnotes is a dictation notepad: open it, talk, then copy or share the text. It is a standalone app, so it does not insert text into the app you are in. We did not verify its current price or language list today.

Verdict: good for simple voice notes, not for tap-anywhere dictation.

Who should pick what

  • Quick messages at no cost: Google voice typing or Gboard.
  • Long-form writing you already do with Wispr on desktop: Wispr Flow.
  • Mostly typing, sometimes dictating: SwiftKey.
  • Meetings and interviews: Otter.ai.
  • Translation, rewriting, and a bubble plus keyboard: DictoKey.
